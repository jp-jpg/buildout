How the engine thinks

From 156 years of data to one sized call, in six stages

Every month the engine reads 156 years of data, works out what kind of market this is, estimates what the next year is likely to pay, chooses how much stock a cautious investor would hold, and trusts that answer only as far as its record since 1940 has earned. Each stage is a fitted object with a stated optimiser; the numbers are today's.

    In a fund

    Where this plugs in, and what it produces on day one

    The engine is built to sit beside a portfolio manager and a risk function, not to replace either. It reads the book, states its rules in advance, sizes against a loss limit, grades every figure by its evidence and leaves the trade to a person.

    It consumes
    • Public data: FRED, SEC XBRL filings, Treasury par yields, Shiller's long history; Databento for the tape under a secret key.
    • The fund's own book and limits, kept in a private, git-ignored file the page never sees. Here the book is a model, not personal positions.
    • Stated rules: the loss budget (expected shortfall, worst month in twenty), the lag, the refit schedule, the risk aversion. All listed, with reasons, on the Parameters desk.
    It produces
    • A dated decision record each day: inputs with sources, the regime, the forecasts, the sized call, the gate, what would flip it, and anything blocked.
    • Proposals, never orders: a person approves every trade. The MCP server exposes 34 tools, none of which can place, route or cancel an order, so the fund's own agents and risk systems query the same engine.
    • Evidence-graded figures (observed, estimated, assumed, hypothesis) and a journal that scores past calls for skill against luck.
    How I work
    • Frame the question as a decision under a loss limit, not a forecast: what size, what hedge, and what evidence would change it.
    • Write the rule before looking at the outcome; test it on 156 years, walked forward, against the best of n useless strategies; let the record set how far to trust it.
    • Publish the parameters and the code. A number nobody can trace is not a number the book should act on.
    • Where this sits in a team: between the portfolio manager and risk, turning stated rules into tested code and keeping the record. These desks are the working method, not a demo.
    First thirty days
    • Week one: the fund's book and limits into the private config; the daily record starts, with the fund's own hedges in the hedge selector.
    • Weeks two to three: the fund's standing rules (trend, volatility, valuation) through the labs harness, so each carries a p-value corrected for the variants tried.
    • Week four: the gate calibrated to the fund's own record, with every rule the fund already runs counted among the strategies tried; the parameters desk becomes the risk function's audit page.

            Exposure drivers and flip points

                Matrix methods

                The engine as matrix multiplication: method, speed and equivalence

                A model cannot read a question or observe a market directly. It represents both as matrices and multiplies them against the matrices it has learned. One multiplication can carry thousands of connections at once, which is why chips built to multiply grids now run AI, and why this engine was rebuilt around the same move: the mind thinks about many questions as one product, the regime filter runs as a scan of matrix products instead of a month-by-month loop, and the brain answers every shock against every holding as one sum of matrix powers. The answers are the same; the work is shorter.

                How the mind multiplies

                Queries as vectors, knowledge as a matrix

                The mind's 9,426 ideas, markets, facts and quotes are the rows and columns of one matrix P: entry (j, i) is how much of node i's attention flows to node j along their links. A question lights up a few nodes, a vector s. One step of thinking is one product, r = (1 - a) s + a P r, repeated forty times. Ask twenty-seven questions and they become the twenty-seven columns of a matrix S, and every step is one product P R for all of them: the same arithmetic, done side by side, as a graphics chip does for a neural network.

                The scan

                The regime filter as a parallel scan

                The regime filter carries yesterday's odds into today's: odds(t) = odds(t - 1) times A times the month's evidence. Written out, the odds for every month are running products of matrices, and products can be grouped in any order. So in round one each month multiplies in its neighbour's matrix, in round two the product from two months back, then four, eight, and so on: after eleven rounds (2 to the 11th is more than 1,868 months) every month's odds are known, each round being one batched matrix multiplication. The backward pass runs the same scan from the end, and every random start of the fit runs in the same batch.

                Pearl · when the matrix math is the mining

                Pearl: proof of work as AI inference

                Prompted by DeFi Education's October 22 note. Traditional proof of work spends electricity on hashes that secure the chain and do nothing else. Pearl asks miners for matrix multiplications instead, the work AI inference already does, so one computation can serve a customer and win a block. If that holds, an AI provider earns twice from the same chips and can charge less for inference.

                What the sources say
                  Why it matters for this book, and what to watch
                  • Demand for matrix math. Every design that pays for matrix multiplication, inference or mining, is demand for the chips the book owns (NVIDIA and Broadcom) and for the power and data centers the buildout desks track.
                  • Cheaper inference. If mining rewards subsidize inference, the price of a token of AI falls; cheaper inference tends to raise usage, which is the bull case for the buildout's revenue.
                  • What has to be true. Verification of the work must stay cheap, buyers must pay for the useful output (there was no PRL-priced inference market as of June), and issuance is front-loaded (about half the supply in four years), so the reward's value is the open question.
                  • What would change the view. Paying inference demand on Pearl at scale, or a verification failure. Neither is in the engine's data yet; this is a thesis to watch, not a signal.

                  Market geometry

                  Market structure describes risk well and forecasts returns poorly

                  Covariance geometry

                  150 years of risk structure

                  Every 36 months of stocks, 10-year Treasuries and gold has a covariance matrix: three volatilities and three correlations. Those matrices are not points on a flat sheet; they live on a curved space where the natural distance between two of them (the affine-invariant distance, the size of the logarithm of A−1/2 B A−1/2) asks how much one era's risk must be stretched to become another's, whatever the units. Flatten that space with the matrix logarithm, measure every year against every other year at once (one Gram matrix), and draw the result with classical multidimensional scaling: each dot is a year, and dots close together had risk that looked alike.

                  Analogs, and the placebo that tests them

                  Nearest historical analogs and the placebo test

                  Nearest past windows by the shape of their risk (none overlapping today's, 36 months apart)
                  The decision engine's analog model against its placebos

                  The engine's new return-density model reads next month from the year after each of the eight nearest windows. It scores better than the long-run average, but so do eight windows drawn at random: a mixture of several pasts has fatter tails than one bell curve, and that is the whole gain. The engine keeps the model for its tails and shrinks each analog's mean three quarters of the way to the long run.

                  Nearest by whole distribution: sliced Wasserstein distance over 64 random directions

                  Optimal transport compares the full joint distribution of the three returns, tails included, not only their covariance. Every window's projections are sorted once and compared in one broadcast.

                  Random matrix theory

                  Two significant common factors in global markets

                  A correlation matrix of N series estimated from T months is noisy even when nothing is related: its eigenvalues spread out in a band whose edges Marchenko and Pastur derived from N/T alone. Eigenvalues that rise above the band are real common factors; the rest is the noise of a finite sample. The absorption ratio, the share of all variance the top fifth of eigenvectors carries, measures how tightly markets move as one.

                  Eigenvalues today (bars) against the noise density (curve)

                    Absorption ratio, rolling five years

                    A jump of more than one standard deviation above its recent level has preceded a 10% fall within a year more often than not jumping has, but not by enough to rule out luck once the overlapping years are permuted in blocks (see the tests below).

                    Persistent homology

                    The topology of cross-market correlation

                    Treat each market as a point, with distance √(2(1 − ρ)) from its correlation ρ to every other, and grow a ball around every point. As the balls grow, markets join into clusters (H0, recorded by the minimum spanning tree) and rings appear and fill in (H1, found by reducing the boundary matrix of the Vietoris-Rips complex over the two-element field). Features that last across many scales are structure; flickers are noise.

                    Memory and compounding

                    Persistence and compounding: returns, volatility and drag

                    Detrended fluctuation analysis measures how a series' wiggle grows with the time scale: a Hurst exponent of 0.5 is a random walk. Shiller's monthly prices are averages of daily closes, which smooths returns and lifts the exponent, so read the eras against each other more than against 0.5. The compounding table is the other geometry of returns: what a holder earns is the average log return, which falls short of the average return by about half the variance.

                    Results after multiple-testing correction

                    Each claim is walked forward with only what was known at the time, scored against a simple benchmark with errors that respect overlapping years, and its p-value is adjusted with the others by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.                         Price of protection

                        Implied versus realized volatility

                        Implied volatility is what an option charges; realized volatility is what the market delivered. The gap is the volatility risk premium: on average buyers of protection pay sellers for carrying crash risk. The desk buys convexity when it is cheap, not when it is needed.

                        Desk rules: buy convexity when implied sits in the bottom third of its ten-year range and within about two points of its median premium; prefer put spreads and collars when implied is in the top fifth; when the VIX curve inverts, stress is priced now, so monetize in stages instead of buying.

                                          How fragile is each measured link?

                                          Self-checks

                                          Model reliability

                                          Do its 90% error bars hold? Every month predicted from earlier months only, 2000 on

                                          Textbook: the ensemble's Gaussian interval. Conformal: adaptive conformal inference (Gibbs and Candès, 2021), whose width is the 90% quantile of past standardized errors and whose target adapts after every miss, so coverage stays near 90% even when the world shifts. "Honest width now" is the conformal half-width over the textbook one.

                                          Which world model the data trusts, scored out of sample

                                          Five rival models of each measured equation: full-history least squares, the last 36 months only, an outlier-resistant Huber fit, links that drift (Kalman filter), and no link at all. Stacking (Yao, Vehtari, Simpson and Gelman, 2018) picks the mixture with the best out-of-sample log score; each month's weights use earlier months only.

                                          What would an unseen common cause have to explain to erase each link?

                                          The robustness value (Cinelli and Hazlett, 2020): the share of the residual variance of both the parent and the child that a confounder would have to explain to drive the link to zero, and in brackets to make it insignificant at 5%. Compare it with the strongest observed parent: a hidden cause stronger than anything measured is possible, but it is a claim to argue, not a default.

                                                                                In one sentence

                                                                                What the engine does every month

                                                                                Every month the engine reads 156 years of market data, works out what kind of market this is, estimates what the next year is likely to pay, chooses how much stock a cautious investor would hold given that, and then trusts its own answer only as far as its track record since 1940 has earned.

                                                                                In one paragraph

                                                                                The same thing, with the moving parts named

                                                                                The engine is a set of numbers learned from history, with the rules that update them written down in advance. It starts from monthly US stock returns since 1871 and a handful of things known at the time: how expensive stocks are, where bond yields and inflation stand, how the market has been trending, how volatile it has been and how far it sits below its last high. From these it keeps two kinds of belief up to date. One is about the state of the market: a few regimes (calm, turbulent, stressed), each with its own typical return and risk, and the odds of being in each this month. The other is about the next year: four simple forecasters, combined by how well each has actually done.

                                                                                It then asks one question. For an investor who dislikes losses about as much as the average long-run investor must have (the dislike is set so that holding exactly one times capital is the neutral answer), what exposure to stocks makes the next month's expected outcome best, provided the worst month in twenty costs no more than 12% of capital? That number is the engine's optimum, 1.50x today.

                                                                                Between that optimum and the book sits a gate. The engine moves the book away from plain holding only in proportion to how strong its own record since 1940 is, after discounting for every version of itself that was tried. Today that record is not strong enough, so the gate is 0% and the book holds 1.00x. Two standing guards sit alongside: a hedge selector that chooses between all stocks, a Treasury sleeve and a cash sleeve by their recent record in the current regime, and a trend overlay that would scale exposure when the market is below its 10-month average, adopted only if the scaling survives the same walk-forward test the engine applies to itself. Today the overlay is tested and not adopted, because it failed the same walk-forward test the engine applies to itself.

                                                                                  How to read it

                                                                                  Why the decision core is small, and why that is the point

                                                                                  Learned against set

                                                                                  A parameter is learned when an algorithm chose its value to optimise a stated objective on stated data; it is set when a person chose it. The engine keeps the two apart because they fail differently: a learned value drifts with the data and is caught by the refit schedule and the walk-forward record; a set value is a judgment and is caught only by a reader who disagrees. Every row above says which it is.

                                                                                  Why so few numbers decide

                                                                                  The decision core is a few thousand numbers because the targets are noisy and the history, though long, is one history. Each forecaster is refit every two years on a few hundred effective observations; a model with many more parameters than that would fit the noise, which is what the machine-learning lab finds when it tries. The one large object, the knowledge graph's spectral map, is not a forecast: it compresses what the system knows so that every question becomes a matrix product, and it is judged by retrieval, not by returns.

                                                                                  Walked forward, with a lag

                                                                                  A value used at month t was fit on data through t minus two. The refit schedule is part of the parameter: the regime models refit every five years (eighteen fits since 1940), the forecasters every two, the recursive objects every month. The gate then decides how much of the engine's optimum reaches the book, from the t statistic of its record against the best of eight useless strategies.

                                                                                          Tool · what-if

                                                                                          Belief network: correlated claims

                                                                                          Thirteen of the claims the mind weighs are linked by what the atlas learned: a one-standard-deviation shock to each claim's own market series, and how every other claim's series responds over twelve months. Each claim starts from its own evidence; mean-field belief propagation then lets connected claims pull on each other. Set one and watch the rest re-settle. Ask "What if oil spikes, what happens to the book?" above for the same thing in words.

                                                                                          How it thinks

                                                                                          The reasoning loop, step by step

                                                                                          The same code runs in Python (buildout/mind.py) and on this page (mind.js); a test holds them equal on every push.

                                                                                          1. Read. The question's words are lower-cased, phrases mapped ("digital gold" to bitcoin and store of value), stop words dropped, endings trimmed ("rates" to "rate") and synonyms added ("NVDA" to Nvidia). Filler words such as "useful" or "work" are ignored for recall.
                                                                                          2. Recall. Every node has a document: its name three times, its other names twice, its definition or text once. BM25, the ranking formula search engines use, scores all {N} documents against the question's words; a node whose exact name appears in the question gets a bonus. Long kinds of node (quotes, signals, investors) are damped so a long text cannot win on length. Up to twelve seeds are kept, each scoring at least 30% of the best.
                                                                                          3. Spread. Spreading activation: a random walker starts at the seeds and, at each of 40 steps, follows a link with probability 0.75 (weighted by the link's type: "is a" counts more than "related") or jumps back to a seed. Where it spends its time is how relevant each node is to the question. This is personalized PageRank; two or three links away still lights up if it is well connected.
                                                                                          4. Frame. Each claim the mind can weigh is an axis: the 18 investor-voices topics (stated as a bull case and a bear case) and seven "does this method give an edge?" questions. The axis whose evidence lit up most wins, with a boost if the voices library also picks that topic. The wording decides which side is the claim: "Is AI a bubble?" weighs the bear case. "What is X?" adds X's definition; "What if X, what happens to Y?" sets X's claim in the belief network and weighs Y, or the model book.
                                                                                          5. Weigh. Each signal on that axis adds sign × strength × reliability × relevance to the log-odds of the bull case. Reliability is how well that kind of evidence has predicted out of sample, or how certain the fact is. Relevance is 0.5 to 1 by how much that signal lit up. Within one family of evidence the second reading counts 1/√2, the third 1/√3, so ten restatements of one fact count about five times one reading, not ten. One more row is the pull of connected claims through the belief network, capped so it informs a verdict and never decides it. Investor opinion is one more, low-reliability signal. Probability = 1 / (1 + e−log-odds).
                                                                                          6. Test. Each family of evidence is left out in turn; the spread of the verdict is the range shown. The single signal whose removal moves the verdict most is the hinge, and every signal states the reading that would flip it.
                                                                                          7. Trace. From the market nodes the evidence rests on, the strongest directed path to the book over the atlas's own learned and judged links (Dijkstra on −log of each link's size), with its sign.
                                                                                          8. Decide. If the evidence leans against the book on that claim, it names the matching hedge in the hedge book and writes the two MCP calls a person would review; otherwise it holds. It never places an order.

                                                                                              Machine-learning lab

                                                                                              Return forecasting: only the simplest model outperforms the historical mean

                                                                                              Seven models, from one-variable CAPE regression to gradient boosting, forecast the S&P 500's next twelve months each month since 1940, refit only on what was known at the time. The flexible ones lose to the plain historical average out of sample. The one that wins is the simplest: an average of eight one-variable forecasts with Campbell and Thompson's sign restrictions, by a small margin that is not yet statistically significant, which is what Rapach, Strauss and Zhou found on other data. A reinforcement-learning sizing agent, judged the same way, does not beat holding either. That is the useful finding: it says which model claims to distrust and why the engine leans on ranges, hedges and sizing rules instead of point forecasts.

                                                                                              Scoreboard

                                                                                              Out-of-sample model scores

                                                                                              Skill is how much smaller each model's squared error is than the historical mean's (Campbell and Thompson's out-of-sample R squared): above zero beats the mean, below zero loses to it. Features are the eight readings a person could have had that month: valuation, the excess CAPE yield, the long rate, inflation, momentum, the distance from the 10-month average, volatility and the drawdown.

                                                                                                Overfitting

                                                                                                In-sample leaders underperform out of sample

                                                                                                Combinatorial purged cross-validation (López de Prado) cuts the record into eight blocks, trains on six and tests on two, all 28 ways, with a purge so no test month's outcome overlaps a training month's. The probability of backtest overfitting is how often the model that looked best in training ranks below the median in testing. Gradient boosting fits the past best and forecasts the future worst: the textbook signature of overfitting.

                                                                                                Share of the accuracy lost when one feature is shuffled in the test blocks; a negative share means the model does better without it.

                                                                                                Calibration and intervals

                                                                                                Crash-probability calibration

                                                                                                A forecast is only usable if its probabilities mean what they say. The crash ensemble is roughly right at low readings and overconfident at high ones. The return intervals are split-conformal: their width is the 80th percentile of the last 30 years' forecast errors, which needs no assumption about the shape of returns, and they covered the realized return about as often as promised.

                                                                                                Analogs and novelty

                                                                                                Historical analogs and current novelty

                                                                                                Nearest neighbours on the same standardized features, no two within 18 months of each other. The eight nearest months run from July 1929 to October 2018, and they split evenly: four were followed by a fall of 15% or more within a year (1929, 1966, 2000 and 2007) and four by gains. Novelty measures how far today sits from its 24 nearest months against how far a typical month sits from its own; a model is extrapolating when today is further out than most of history.

                                                                                                Reinforcement learning

                                                                                                Reinforcement-learning sizing, judged out of sample

                                                                                                Each month the agent picks 0, 0.5, 1 or 1.5 times capital in stocks from what it could know: the trend (a month late), volatility and valuation terciles set on earlier data only, the drawdown and its own last choice. Its reward is the month's log growth after trading and borrowing costs, Kelly's objective, with an optional penalty on losses. Because one investor's trades do not move the market, this is a contextual bandit with switching costs, and fitted Q iteration on the record solves it exactly; thin states are shrunk toward the all-month average so it cannot memorize a handful of months. Retrained every year from 1940 and compared with holding and the 10-month trend rule on the same months.

                                                                                                Use

                                                                                                Implications for portfolio management

                                                                                                1. Distrust any return forecast that has not been scored like this. Purged, walk-forward, against the historical mean, with a Diebold-Mariano test. Most published predictors fail it (Goyal, Welch and Zafirov); here, every flexible model does, and the one combination that passes does so by a margin that is not yet significant.
                                                                                                2. Prefer the simple model when the complex one only wins in sample. The restricted combination of one-variable forecasts is the only model above the mean out of sample; in cross-validation gradient boosting fits the past best and the future worst. Complexity bought fit, not foresight.
                                                                                                3. Use ML for what it is good at here: ranges, analogs and novelty. The conformal interval says how wide the range is; the analogs say which histories to read; novelty says when every model is extrapolating.
                                                                                                4. Size from ranges, not points. A forecast of +2.8% with an 80% interval of ±23% is a statement about uncertainty, and the sizing and hedge desks are built for that.

                                                                                                Reality check

                                                                                                Model assumptions tested, with each conclusion's breakpoint

                                                                                                A common portfolio-management concern is that quantitative models rest on assumptions that fail in live markets. The remedy is not a better model. It is to write down what each model assumes, test the assumption on the data every day, know how wrong it can be before the conclusion changes, rerun the decision in worlds where it fails, and haircut every backtest for what backtests usually lose live.

                                                                                                Backtest-to-live decay

                                                                                                Enter the pitch. The triage takes off trading costs, then the decay McLean and Pontiff measured on 97 published predictors (26% lower out of sample, 58% lower after publication), applied to the Sharpe ratio with volatility unchanged, then asks whether the Sharpe beats the best of the variants tried by luck alone (the deflated Sharpe ratio, Bailey and López de Prado), and whether the backtest is long enough for that many tries (the minimum backtest length). Skill-less variants are assumed to scatter by the sampling noise of a Sharpe ratio, one over the square root of the years.

                                                                                                Verdict
                                                                                                Seven questions for any model pitch
                                                                                                1. How many versions did you try before this one? The answer sets the luck hurdle; "one" is almost never true.
                                                                                                2. What is the economic reason it should work, written before the test? A reason found after the result is a description of the result.
                                                                                                3. Was the test purged and walk-forward, with no future data in any feature? Revised macro data, survivorship and overlapping labels are the usual leaks.
                                                                                                4. What does it assume about tails, independence and stability, and what does it do when those fail? Ask for the breakpoint, not the base case.
                                                                                                5. What are the costs at the size we would run, and the capacity? Most high-turnover anomalies do not survive costs (Novy-Marx and Velikov).
                                                                                                6. Who else runs it? Crowded models fail together, as the August 2007 quant funds did (Khandani and Lo).
                                                                                                7. What result in the next six months would turn it off? A model without a kill rule is an opinion with a backtest.

                                                                                                Breakpoints

                                                                                                Assumption breakpoints

                                                                                                Every conclusion has a number at which it flips. Knowing that number turns "the model is wrong" into "the model is wrong by more than X", which can be checked.

                                                                                                The TLT hedge: the correlation line

                                                                                                Adding a sleeve of weight w lowers risk only while ρ < −w·σbond / (2σbook). The engine's hedge policy watches this line, and the research step decides when the correlation regime has turned.

                                                                                                The trend rule: Sharpe gain over holding, by how late it acts

                                                                                                Shiller's monthly averages make returns look more trending than they were; acting a month late removes most of that. The gain is gone by four months, and at about 56bp a switch.

                                                                                                Tests

                                                                                                Common modelling assumptions

                                                                                                Fat tails

                                                                                                The tail index is a Hill estimate on the largest losses; below 4 the fourth moment, and with it the usual error bars on volatility, is unreliable.

                                                                                                Taleb's kappa: how much data the tails cost you

                                                                                                κ = 2 − (log n − log n0) / log(M(n)/M(n0)), with M the mean absolute deviation of a sum of n draws (Taleb 2019). The Gaussian row is a check: it should read 0.

                                                                                                Correlations in the worst months

                                                                                                Diversification measured on all months overstates what it does when it is needed (Longin and Solnik 2001; Ang and Chen 2002).

                                                                                                Stressed worlds

                                                                                                Decisions under counterfactual histories

                                                                                                Four hundred 50-year histories, each stitched from 12-month blocks of the real joint record of stocks, bonds and inflation (a stationary bootstrap, so crashes and their aftermaths stay together). Then again drawing only from inflationary months. A conclusion that holds in one set and fails in the other is a conclusion about the regime, not about the asset.

                                                                                                Fragility

                                                                                                Convexity of portfolio losses

                                                                                                Taleb and Douady's heuristic: shift the market by s, then by s ± 5%, and compare. If the average of the two neighbours is worse than the middle, losses accelerate (fragile); if better, they slow (convex). Priced on the hedge book's own option model.

                                                                                                Selling puts earns income in calm markets and turns fragile in the tail. The collar keeps the book convex across the range; a put spread is convex down to its lower strike and fragile again beyond it.

                                                                                                Tool · Damodaran

                                                                                                The implied equity risk premium

                                                                                                The discount rate that makes the index equal to the present value of the cash it is expected to return: five years of growth, then growth at the risk-free rate forever. Subtract the risk-free rate and what is left is the premium the market is pricing in. It needs no forecast of the index, only of cash flows.

                                                                                                Tool · Damodaran

                                                                                                Narrative to numbers: the story the price requires

                                                                                                Damodaran's method ties every input to a story: revenue growth for five years that fades to the risk-free rate by year ten, an operating margin that converges to a target, reinvestment set by a sales-to-capital ratio, a cost of capital that drifts to a mature level, terminal growth capped at the risk-free rate, and a chance the story fails. The defaults are a chip designer at the semiconductor industry's own January 2026 averages from his data pages.

                                                                                                Decision math

                                                                                                Decision theory from poker, bargaining and storage

                                                                                                Trading firms use poker in training because it builds the same disciplines: think in ranges, price every call against the pot, size bets so variance cannot end the game, and judge decisions by their odds rather than their outcome. Negotiation, private-fund cash flows and the commodity curve have their own exact math. Each calculator runs the engine's functions (buildout/games2.py), the same in Python and here.

                                                                                                Poker: equity and pot odds

                                                                                                A seven-card evaluator picks each player's best five; equity counts wins plus shared pots. Call when equity beats call ÷ (pot + call). The same seeded generator runs in Python, so the answer is reproducible.

                                                                                                Kelly sizing and the risk of ruin

                                                                                                Kelly (1956): stake p − (1 − p)/b. Twice Kelly gives zero long-run growth; ruin odds use the diffusion approximation e−2μB/σ².

                                                                                                Bargaining

                                                                                                Rubinstein (1982): the opener's share is (1 − d₂)/(1 − d₁d₂). The ask that maximizes expected surplus against a walk-away spread evenly between low and high is (high + your walk-away)/2. Make the first offer when you know the range (Galinsky and Mussweiler 2001); let them open when you do not.

                                                                                                Commodities: the theory of storage

                                                                                                F = S·e(r + u − y)T (Kaldor 1939, Working 1949). The defaults use mid-October's Dated Brent near $134 and futures near $107 (IEA); the expiry gap is illustrative.

                                                                                                Private markets

                                                                                                Private fund J-curve (Takahashi-Alexander)

                                                                                                Calls are a share of what is still uncommitted, distributions a rising share of NAV; the investor's net cash dips before it recovers. IRR, TVPI, DPI and the Kaplan-Schoar PME are in the engine for any cash flows.
